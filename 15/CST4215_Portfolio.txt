Why root mean square is used to measure model performance: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is used because it measures a model's prediction errors in the exact same units as the target variable while penalizing large mistakes more severely.
                                                            When you predict something like house prices, an RMSE of 50,000 means your predictions are, roughly speaking, off by $50,000.
                                                            Here is the exact breakdown of why RMSE is a preferred metric in machine learning and statistics:
                                                            ## 1. It is in the "Real World" Unit
                                                            To calculate RMSE, the mathematical formula squares the errors first (which turns them into squared dollars, squared meters, etc.) and then takes the square root at the very end. Taking the square root converts the metric back into the original unit of your data, making it highly interpretable for humans.
                                                            ## 2. It Heavily Penalizes Large Errors
                                                            Because the errors are squared before they are averaged, large errors have a disproportionately massive impact on the final score.

                                                            * A small mistake of 2 units becomes a penalty of 4 ($2^2$).
                                                            * A large mistake of 10 units becomes a penalty of 100 ($10^2$).

                                                            This makes RMSE the perfect metric when large mistakes are disastrous for your project, and you want a model that avoids huge misses at all costs.
                                                            ------------------------------
                                                            ## RMSE vs. MAE (Mean Absolute Error)
                                                            Data scientists frequently choose between RMSE and MAE. A direct comparison shows when to use each:

                                                            | Metric ------------------------| How it handles errors -----------------------------------------------------| Best used when... -------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
                                                            |--------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
                                                            | RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) | Penalizes large errors heavily (due to squaring).  ------------------------| Large errors are highly undesirable or dangerous (e.g., flight delay predictions). --------------|
                                                            | MAE (Mean Absolute Error) ---- | Treats all errors linearly (an error of 10 is just 10 times worse than 1). | You want a stable average error and your data contains outliers you don't want to over-penalize. |

                                                            ## Mathematical Advantage
                                                            For advanced machine learning, RMSE is mathematically differentiable because it relies on squaring rather than absolute values (which create sharp corners on a graph). This smooth mathematical profile makes it much easier for optimization algorithms (like Gradient Descent) to calculate gradients and train models efficiently.

Changind random state chasing the perfect r-squared: No, you should never adjust the random_state just to hunt for a higher R-squared score.
                                                        Doing this is a dangerous practice known as "random state hacking" or data snooping.
                                                        Here is why changing the random_state alters your score, and why you should avoid tweaking it to get a "perfect" result:
                                                        ## Why changing random_state changes the score
                                                        When you change the random_state, you shuffle the dataset differently. This means your training set gets a slightly different mix of data points, and your test set gets a different mix.

                                                        * If your score goes up, it usually just means your model got lucky and was evaluated on an "easy" test set that happened to perfectly align with what it learned.
                                                        * It does not mean your model actually became smarter or better at predicting real-world data.

                                                        ## The Danger: Overfitting to the Split
                                                        If you manually test 50 different random states and pick the one that gives you an $R^2$ of 0.95, your model hasn't improved. You have simply overfit your model selection to a specific random split. The moment you deploy this model or feed it fresh, real-world data, its performance will likely crash back down.
                                                        ------------------------------
                                                        ## What to do instead: Cross-Validation
                                                        If your R-squared score jumps around significantly when you change the random_state, it means your model's performance is highly unstable and dependent on luck.
                                                        Instead of searching for a perfect random_state, you should use K-Fold Cross-Validation.

                                                        from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score# This splits the data 5 different ways, calculates R2 for each, and averages themscores = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5, scoring='r2')

                                                        Cross-validation eliminates the luck factor by splitting your data multiple times (e.g., 5 or 10 times), testing the model on every single piece of data exactly once, and averaging the scores. An average cross-validation $R^2$ score gives you the true, honest performance of your model.
